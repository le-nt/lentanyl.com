THE HUMAN CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM must stay continuously engaged with goal-driven activity in order to sustain stable cognitive and affective functioning. This is a biological constraint, supported by what we currently understand about dopaminergic reward circuitry, which is structured not around the attainment of goals but around the pursuit of them. When this drive is denied expression and an organism capable of intricate problem-solving is placed in an environment that offers neither problems to solve nor agency over its own circumstances, the system will generate its own objects of attention. Like an iris in the dark expanding to let in more light, the mind expands its search for acceptable information, lowers its standards and finds patterns in noise, stories in random occurrence, and meaning in the incidental features of whatever environment it has been confined to. When structured input is removed, the brain will hallucinate structure. Plummeting into the absence of purposeful activity, the brain will contrive purpose, desperately latching on to whatever substrate is available to a vivid degree that feels indistinguishable from genuine discovery. These conditions, and the so-called surrogate activities they inevitably produce, have become psychotic ambient features of industrialized life. Features generated not by deliberate prescription but by the accumulated logic of economic and technological systems that solve for efficiency, comfort, and risk reduction while inadvertently stripping from daily life the very thing the nervous system cannot do without. We are, as a species, parsing wallpaper at extraordinary scale.
